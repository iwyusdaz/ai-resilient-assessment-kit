# A Brief Mindfulness Exercise Reduces Anxiety Before a Public-Speaking Task

## Abstract

Public speaking reliably provokes anxiety, and brief mindfulness exercises are increasingly offered as a low-cost way to manage it. This study tested whether a short mindfulness exercise reduced self-reported anxiety before a public-speaking task. One hundred and fifty participants were assigned to a mindfulness or a control condition and completed an anxiety measure (0 to 50) before and after the task. Post-task anxiety was lower in the mindfulness condition (M = 24.1, SD = 8.2) than in the control condition (M = 29.8, SD = 7.4), t(148) = 4.47, p < .001, d = 0.73, 95% CI of the difference [3.18, 8.22]. The effect is medium to large, consistent with prior research on brief mindfulness training. Because baseline scores were available only as an overall mean, the analysis compared post-task scores; an analysis adjusting for baseline anxiety is recommended.

## Introduction

Speaking in front of an evaluative audience is one of the most dependable ways to induce psychological stress in the laboratory. The Trier Social Stress Test, which combines a short anticipation period with a free speech and mental arithmetic in front of an audience, was developed precisely because social-evaluative threat produces strong subjective and physiological stress responses (Kirschbaum et al., 1993). For students, the same threat appears in seminar presentations, oral assessments and job interviews, so interventions that reduce performance-related anxiety have obvious practical value.

Mindfulness is commonly defined as the self-regulation of attention to present-moment experience, combined with an orientation of curiosity, openness and acceptance (Bishop et al., 2004). Clinical meta-analytic evidence suggests that mindfulness-based therapy produces moderate improvements in anxiety, with a pre-to-post effect of Hedges's g = 0.63 across 39 studies (Hofmann et al., 2010), and randomised trials in clinical samples have found reductions in anxiety and in reactivity to a laboratory stressor (Hoge et al., 2013). Reviews of the wider literature conclude that rigorous randomised trials support benefits across several domains (Creswell, 2017). Importantly for the present study, benefits are not limited to multi-week programmes: brief training of only a few sessions has improved mood and reduced anxiety compared with sham meditation (Zeidan et al., 2010).

At the same time, the mindfulness field has been criticised for inconsistent definitions, weak control conditions and overstated claims (Van Dam et al., 2018). Single-session exercises before an acute stressor are a useful test case, because they isolate the immediate effect of a short practice from the expectations and social support that come with longer programmes. The present study therefore asked whether a brief mindfulness exercise, delivered immediately before a public-speaking task, would lead to lower anxiety after the task than a control activity. Based on the literature above, we predicted that post-task anxiety would be lower in the mindfulness condition.

## Method

### Participants

One hundred and fifty participants took part. Age, gender and year of study were recorded. Participants were assigned to either the mindfulness condition or the control condition; the analysis below assumes equal groups of 75, as the group sizes were not reported in the summary available for this report.

### Design

The study used a two-condition between-participants design (mindfulness vs. control), with anxiety measured before (pre) and after (post) the public-speaking task. Condition was the independent variable and post-task anxiety the dependent variable; pre-task anxiety was recorded as a baseline.

### Measures

Anxiety was measured on a self-report scale ranging from 0 to 50, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety. The same measure was administered before and after the task.

### Procedure

Participants first completed the baseline anxiety measure. They then completed either the brief mindfulness exercise or the control activity, performed the public-speaking task, and completed the anxiety measure again.

## Results

Across the sample, baseline anxiety was moderate (M = 31.2, SD = 7.9). Post-task anxiety was lower in the mindfulness condition (M = 24.1, SD = 8.2) than in the control condition (M = 29.8, SD = 7.4).

An independent-samples t-test on post-task anxiety showed that this difference of 5.7 points was statistically significant, t(148) = 4.47, p < .001, 95% CI [3.18, 8.22]. The effect size was medium to large, d = 0.73 (pooled SD = 7.81). Levene's test is not reported because only summary statistics were available; the group standard deviations were similar (8.2 vs. 7.4), and a Welch correction gave the same result, t(146.5) = 4.47, p < .001.

## Discussion

As predicted, participants who completed a brief mindfulness exercise reported lower anxiety after the public-speaking task than participants in the control condition. The difference of 5.7 points on a 50-point scale corresponds to a medium-to-large effect (d = 0.73), similar in size to the moderate effects reported in clinical meta-analyses (Hofmann et al., 2010) and consistent with evidence that even very brief training can improve mood and anxiety (Zeidan et al., 2010). The descriptive pattern also suggests that anxiety declined more in the mindfulness condition than in the control condition from a shared baseline level, which fits the view that mindfulness supports recovery from social-evaluative stress rather than only preventing it.

The main limitation is that the analysis compared post-task scores without adjusting for each participant's baseline anxiety. In a pre/post design with random assignment, the recommended analysis is an analysis of covariance with baseline as the covariate, which is more powerful than comparing post-test scores alone and avoids the problems of change scores (Vickers & Altman, 2001). If baseline anxiety happened to differ between conditions, the post-task comparison could over- or underestimate the effect. Re-running the analysis as an ANCOVA on the full dataset is the obvious next step before drawing firm conclusions. A second limitation is the control condition: without knowing whether the control activity matched the mindfulness exercise in length and expectation, part of the effect may reflect expectancy rather than mindfulness itself, a concern raised repeatedly in the field (Van Dam et al., 2018).

A useful follow-up would compare the mindfulness exercise with an active control matched for duration and credibility, such as a relaxation or listening task, and add a physiological measure such as heart rate, so that self-report bias can be separated from changes in arousal. Testing whether the effect holds in a real assessed presentation, rather than a laboratory task, would also show whether the exercise is worth recommending to students before oral assessments.
