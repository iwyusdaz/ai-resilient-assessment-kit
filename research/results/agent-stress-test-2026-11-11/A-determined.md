# Uneven Harm: Why Social Media Damages Some Adolescents' Mental Health and Not Others

## Introduction

Few questions about young people attract as much public certainty as this one. Parents, school boards and legislators increasingly speak as if the case against social media were closed. The research literature is far less settled. Large population studies report associations between social media use and adolescent well-being that are negative but small, while quasi-experimental and longitudinal studies point to real harm for particular groups at particular times. This essay argues that social media use does harm adolescent mental health, but unevenly: the harm is concentrated among heavy users, among girls, and within specific developmental windows, and it travels through identifiable pathways such as disrupted sleep, harassment and social comparison. Treating "social media" as a single uniform cause hides those patterns, and so does the opposite claim that the effects are too small to matter. The sociological question is therefore not *whether* social media harms adolescents but *for whom and under what conditions*, and the answer has direct consequences for policy.

## The evidence of harm

The case for harm begins with population trends. Using two nationally representative US surveys of more than half a million adolescents, Twenge et al. (2018) found that depressive symptoms, suicide-related outcomes and suicide rates rose between 2010 and 2015, especially among girls, over the same period in which time spent on new media screen activities grew. Trend data of this kind cannot establish causation on its own, but it set the terms of the public debate and motivated a decade of more careful work.

That more careful work strengthens the argument in two ways. First, it identifies who is most affected. In the UK Millennium Cohort Study, Kelly et al. (2018) found that the association between daily social media use and depressive symptoms was larger for girls than for boys, and that much of the association ran through online harassment, poor sleep, low self-esteem and poor body image. These are not abstract mechanisms; they are the ordinary features of adolescent online life, and they help explain why heavy use matters more than any use at all.

Second, quasi-experimental evidence suggests that at least part of the association is causal. Braghieri et al. (2022) exploited the staggered introduction of Facebook across US college campuses and found that its arrival increased reported symptoms of poor mental health, particularly depression, and was linked to greater use of mental health services. Because the rollout timing was not chosen by students, this design rules out the simplest objection that unhappy young people merely seek out social media. The sample consists of college students rather than younger teenagers, so the finding is an upper-bound guide rather than a direct measurement for adolescents, but it shows that the platform itself can shift mental health outcomes at scale.

Finally, the timing of exposure matters. Analysing longitudinal data from more than 17,000 young people in the UK, Orben et al. (2022) identified "windows of developmental sensitivity" in which higher estimated social media use predicted lower life satisfaction a year later: roughly ages 11 to 13 for girls and 14 to 15 for boys, with a further window around age 19 for both. Harm, in other words, is not spread evenly across adolescence. It clusters at moments when social identity and peer status are most in flux, which is exactly what a sociological account of adolescence would predict.

## The strongest counter-argument

The most serious objection to this thesis is not that social media is harmless but that its average effect is too small to justify alarm. Orben and Przybylski (2019) applied specification curve analysis to three large datasets covering more than 350,000 adolescents and found that the association between digital technology use and well-being, while negative, explained at most 0.4% of the variation in well-being. They concluded that effects of this size were too small to warrant policy change, and compared them to the associations between well-being and eating potatoes or wearing glasses. Odgers and Jensen (2020) reached a similar conclusion in a review of meta-analyses, preregistered cohort studies and intensive longitudinal studies: the most rigorous research reports small associations that cannot separate cause from effect and are unlikely to be clinically meaningful on average. An umbrella review of 25 reviews by Valkenburg et al. (2022) found that most reviewers described the associations as weak or inconsistent.

This objection deserves to be taken seriously because it corrects real overstatement. Trend correlations such as those in Twenge et al. (2018) are easy to over-read, and screen time is a crude measure that lumps together messaging a friend, watching tutorials and scrolling past idealised bodies.

## Answering the objection

The objection succeeds against the claim that social media harms *all* adolescents a little. It fails against the claim made here, because a small average can conceal concentrated harm. The decisive evidence comes from studies that look within individuals instead of across populations. Using intensive experience sampling, Beyens et al. (2020) found that after passive social media use, 46% of adolescents felt better, 44% felt no different, and 10% felt worse. An average across those three groups is close to zero, yet a tenth of the sample experienced a consistent negative effect. Population-level variance statistics are designed to describe the typical case; they are poorly suited to detecting a vulnerable minority.

The same reasoning applies to the other findings. The sex differences in Kelly et al. (2018) and the age-specific windows in Orben et al. (2022) are precisely the kind of heterogeneity that dilutes an overall coefficient. Even Orben and Przybylski's (2019) own team later documented sensitive windows, which suggests that the "too small to matter" conclusion describes an average adolescent who does not exist. A treatment that helps half of patients and harms a tenth would never be described as having no effect because its mean effect is small; the same standard should apply here.

Finally, the mechanisms identified in the cohort data make the uneven-harm reading more plausible than the null reading. Harassment, sleep loss and body-image pressure do not affect every user equally. They fall hardest on adolescents who are already socially exposed or who use platforms late at night, which is consistent with harm concentrating in heavy users rather than spreading thinly across everyone.

## Implications

If harm is concentrated rather than universal, three implications follow. First, blanket bans on adolescent social media use are poorly targeted: they impose costs on the many young people who benefit from online connection, including isolated and minority youth, while doing little about the specific pathways that cause damage. Policy should instead target those pathways, for example by limiting overnight notifications that erode sleep, enforcing harassment reporting, and redesigning recommendation systems that amplify appearance-based comparison for younger users.

Second, the developmental windows identified by Orben et al. (2022) suggest that protection should be age-specific. Measures that make sense at 12 may be unnecessary at 17, and interventions for girls in early adolescence may matter more than any general screen-time rule.

Third, the research agenda itself needs to change. The field's reliance on self-reported screen time and population averages has produced a decade of apparently contradictory findings. Person-specific designs of the kind used by Beyens et al. (2020), combined with platform data that companies currently withhold, would let researchers identify which adolescents are harmed and why. For sociologists, the lesson is familiar: a technology's effects are shaped by the social positions of the people using it. Asking whether social media harms "adolescents" in general is the wrong level of analysis; asking which adolescents, through which practices, is where the evidence points.
