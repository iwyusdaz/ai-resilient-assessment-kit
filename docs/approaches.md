# Eight approaches compared

A working comparison of eight redesign moves for AI-era assessment. Ratings are our evidence-based judgments for **first- and second-year undergraduate courses** (the kit's scope), not a systematic review. Where a rating is arguable, the "evidence base" column says so — and the [literature notes](https://github.com/iwyusdaz/ai-resilient-assessment-kit/blob/main/research/literature-notes.md) show what was checked.

**Evidence-base key:** **strong** = multiple independent studies or institutional adoption; **emerging** = single-site studies or documented pilots; **anecdotal** = practitioner reports and reasoned argument only.

## The eight approaches

### 1. In-class writing

Writing done live in class (or a scheduled exam slot), unaided by AI.

- **Validity benefit:** strong — the strongest possible link between the student and the words on the page.
- **Workload:** low change to grading, but class time is consumed and drafts cannot be meaningfully iterative in the room.
- **Scalability:** moderate — works in lecture halls with seating spread; grading is the same as any essay.
- **Equity/accessibility risks:** high for students with slow typing, writing disabilities, anxiety, or extra-time accommodations — timing pressure *is* the assessment barrier. Extended time and typing accommodations are mandatory, which strains "everyone writes at once".
- **Student experience:** often negative for second-language writers and deep, slow thinkers; writing quality under time pressure ≠ thinking quality.
- **Evidence base:** emerging — long-standing exam practice, but the AI-era literature on in-class writing as a *redesign* response is mostly practitioner reports.

### 2. Oral checks (viva-lite)

Short structured spoken Q&A: student defends choices, explains their work, answers "why did you do X?".

- **Validity benefit:** strong — the student must *use* the work in real time; the best single check that the artefact is theirs.
- **Workload:** high per student at scale; mitigable by sampling (see below).
- **Scalability:** low without adaptation — 200 students × 5 minutes ≈ 17 hours. Scaling moves: sample (e.g., every 5th student plus anyone whose process evidence raises questions), run in tutorial groups, use TAs with a shared question bank, or offer group-scheduled slots.
- **Equity/accessibility risks:** significant for communication disabilities, anxiety, and non-native speakers — **always provide a written or recorded alternative of equal weight.** Oral checks measure speaking as well as knowing, which is usually not the outcome being assessed.
- **Student experience:** intimidating for some, valuable for others; predictable questions and a published rubric reduce fear.
- **Evidence base:** emerging — institutional adoption at scale is documented (Sydney's secure lane; interactive oral programs), but outcome studies are young.

### 3. Staged submissions

One artefact built in graded stages: proposal → annotated sources → draft → final → short defence.

- **Validity benefit:** strong — the chain of stages produces a traceable process; each stage is cheap to grade.
- **Workload:** moderate upfront (design + deadlines), but per-stage grading is quick with the right rubric; total grading similar to marking one big essay badly.
- **Scalability:** moderate — feedback per stage is the constraint; in large classes, stages can be graded complete/incomplete against checklists with the final stage carrying the grade.
- **Equity/accessibility risks:** low if deadlines are spaced and extensions are real; students who start late get less value but no penalty beyond the late policy.
- **Student experience:** positive for most — checkpoints prevent the last-minute collapse — but students who like working in one burst resist it.
- **Evidence base:** strong — staged/scaffolded assessment is a long-established practice with substantial literature.

### 4. Process portfolios (drafts, logs, version history)

Students submit the artefact **and** the trail: drafts, an AI-use log, a lab notebook, document version history.

- **Validity benefit:** moderate-to-strong — reveals the journey, but a determined fabricator can manufacture a trail; combine with a short defence for assurance.
- **Workload:** moderate — grading focuses on the log/reflection, not re-reading every draft.
- **Scalability:** moderate — reflection and logs grade fast with analytic rubrics; version history is spot-checked, not audited in full.
- **Equity/accessibility risks:** low — asynchronous by nature; watch for students whose lives make "steady documented progress" unrealistic (partial solution: grade the log as evidence, not as a work diary).
- **Student experience:** positive for reflective students; others find it bureaucratic — keep the format short and purposeful.
- **Evidence base:** emerging — portfolios are well established in some fields; their *AI-era* use (logs of AI interaction) is newer and mostly practitioner-documented.

### 5. Personalized or local data

The task runs on data the student (or class) collected: a mini-survey, a local case, their own experiment, this semester's numbers.

- **Validity benefit:** strong — generic AI output can't match specific local context, and the data-collection step itself is assessable.
- **Workload:** moderate — designing a collectable-in-a-week data task is the hard part; grading is comparable to the original.
- **Scalability:** moderate — cohort-level collection (one shared class dataset, student-owned subsamples) scales well; 200 individual projects do not.
- **Equity/accessibility risks:** low-to-moderate — data collection must not require travel, expensive equipment, or access to particular communities; always provide a provided-dataset alternative.
- **Student experience:** generally the most positive of the eight — students see their own data mean something.
- **Evidence base:** emerging — well-grounded in active-learning and lab pedagogy; AI-era framing is new.

### 6. Critiquing AI output

Students are given AI-generated work (or generate some) and must find, fix and justify the errors and weak reasoning.

- **Validity benefit:** strong for critical-evaluation outcomes — the skill of checking AI is itself a learning goal.
- **Workload:** low-to-moderate — the AI draft is the prompt; grading targets the critique.
- **Scalability:** high — one AI artefact serves a whole cohort; critique items are quick to grade.
- **Equity/accessibility risks:** low — asynchronous and text-based; ensure the AI artefact is accessible and the critique format allows structure (bullets, tables) rather than pure prose.
- **Student experience:** engaging — feels like real work. Caveat: students need instruction in *how* to critique, or they find nothing to say.
- **Evidence base:** anecdotal-to-emerging — a fast-moving area; no settled outcome literature yet. (This is stated plainly rather than oversold.)

### 7. Open AI use with reflection

The task invites AI use at **Full AI** level; students document what they used, evaluate the output, and are graded on the quality of their AI-assisted process and reflection.

- **Validity benefit:** strong *if the outcome is AI-collaborative capability* (which is often exactly what a course should teach); weak as an assurance of unaided skill — that's what the secure lane is for.
- **Workload:** low-to-moderate — grading the reflection is additional but light.
- **Scalability:** high — no proctoring, no policing.
- **Equity/accessibility risks:** moderate — paid-tool access varies; levels must be satisfiable with free tiers, and students without any access need an equivalent path. Benefits multilingual students who use AI legitimately as a language aid — *with* disclosure.
- **Student experience:** the most honest match to real-world practice; the risk is students outsourcing learning itself, mitigated by grading the reflection, not the product.
- **Evidence base:** emerging — AIAS deployments (K-10 pilot) and practice reports exist; long-term evidence is not in.

### 8. Proctored/secure checks

Formal supervised assessment: in-person quizzes, tests, exams (the classic secure lane, used sparingly).

- **Validity benefit:** strong for assurance of individual achievement; but it samples performance under constraint, not durable learning.
- **Workload:** high logistics (rooms, invigilation, scheduling), moderate grading (MCQ/short-answer grades fast).
- **Scalability:** moderate-to-high for MCQ-style checks; low for anything requiring spread-out seating at scale.
- **Equity/accessibility risks:** high if used exclusively — exam anxiety, accommodations processing, and the research on exam performance vs. later application all argue for a limited role. Also the least interesting thing to grade.
- **Student experience:** the most hated of the eight, and the least formative.
- **Evidence base:** strong as practice (centuries old), though as an *AI response* it's a retreat, not a design.

## Honesty notes

- These ratings are the kit author's analysis, informed by the sources in the literature notes — not a meta-analysis. The evidence-base column is deliberately coarse.
- Two good approaches can still be a bad combination: stacking all eight into one course produces a workload nobody will sustain (see principle 5).
- The University of Sydney two-lane work (Liu & Bridgeman 2023) is the closest published framework to the "secure a few, open the rest" stance this kit takes.
